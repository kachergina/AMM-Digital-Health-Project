About the project

Research project by Vera Kochergina. Development of the concept of an Automated Medical Module (AMM) for emergency care of patients from socially vulnerable groups.

Introduction

Relevance and objectives

Healthcare systems daily face people pushed to society's margins: homeless individuals, patients with severe alcohol or drug intoxication, those without ID documents, and people lacking basic hygiene. Admission creates infection risks for other patients, aggression toward staff, and diagnostic delays due to missing medical records and advanced untreated illness.

Modern hospitals often lack infrastructure to safely isolate such patients, assess them quickly, and perform sanitary treatment. The AMM concept proposes an isolated gateway for initial assessment, sanitary care, and basic stabilization — always under a doctor's supervision.

Research focus

  • Object: emergency medical care for socially vulnerable patients at hospital admission
  • Subject: organizational and technological concept of a safe isolated module
  • Goal: reduce risks and accelerate initial care while preserving the doctor's leading role

Hypothesis

1. Faster initial assessment (vitals and primary data) vs. standard admission
2. Fewer injuries to healthcare personnel from aggressive or contaminated patients
3. Reduced hospital-acquired infections via isolation and standardized disinfection
4. Increased emergency department capacity through procedure redistribution
Chapter 1

Theoretical background and problem analysis

1.1 Barriers and risks

Estimates suggest tens of thousands of people without stable housing in Russia — the visible tip of a much wider problem. Standard emergency intake is not designed for patients who combine acute need with isolation, safety, and documentation gaps.

Epidemiological barrier

Without dedicated isolation, admission can expose other patients and staff to cross-infection. Clinicians must balance urgent individual care with protecting the wider department.

Behavioral barrier

Stress, intoxication, or confusion can surface as aggression. When staff lack secure distance and support, both safety and morale suffer.

Social-hygienic barrier

Missing documents and severe hygiene barriers slow assessment and block basic clinical access.

Mobile outreach teams provide vital pre-hospital support, but safe, standardized hospital intake remains an unresolved gap.

1.2 Existing medical automation

da Vinci surgical system

Surgeon-controlled robotic instrument with exceptional precision, tremor elimination, and minimally invasive access — proof that manipulators can perform delicate medical procedures.

Hospital service robots

Automated carts at institutions like Moscow's Sklifosovsky Institute transport samples, medications, and linen — freeing nurses for patient care.

UV disinfection robots

Post-COVID widespread use: after staff leave, robots eliminate surface pathogens in minutes — sterility beyond manual cleaning.

1.3 Diagnostics and caregiving concepts

Smart pills — ingestible capsules with miniature cameras capture thousands of intestinal images, transmitting data without invasive endoscopy. ROBEAR (Japan) — a caregiving robot that gently lifts bedridden patients into wheelchairs, reducing nurses' physical strain.

Chapter 1 conclusion: the realistic path is not inventing new technology but integrating proven solutions — robotic manipulators, service robots, UV disinfection, rapid diagnostics — into a safe admission model.

Chapter 2

AMM concept and architecture

2.1 Purpose: a "clean room" at the entrance

AMM is a high-tech gateway between the hazardous outside world and the sterile hospital interior. Container-scale unit next to the emergency department, connected by a sealed corridor. Impact-resistant walls, easy to disinfect, with nothing removable or breakable inside.

Center: multifunctional medical bed-platform. On ceiling rails: a universal robotic manipulator and a diagnostic scanner (cameras, thermal imaging, non-contact sensors). Floor drainage enables automatic cleaning.

2.2 Technology justification

  • Manipulator — inspired by da Vinci, but versatile: catheter, disinfection, blood draw via interchangeable attachments
  • Bed-platform — inspired by ROBEAR: automatic positioning, turning, pressure ulcer prevention
  • Sterilization — mobile UV robot technology integrated for full cycle between patients

2.3 Operating algorithm (6 steps)

1

Admission and isolation

Ambulance team authenticates with electronic card. Patient transferred to bed-platform. External door closes airtight and locks. AMM enters autonomous mode.

2

Non-contact assessment (15–20 sec)

Thermal camera, 3D body model (fractures, injuries), acoustic breathing and speech analysis. Classification: cooperative, unconscious, or aggressive — triggers Protocol Delta if needed.

3

Contact examination

Robotic arm disinfects skin, finds vein via infrared, inserts IV, draws blood for lab-on-a-chip, starts saline, attaches pulse oximeter and BP cuff.

4

Doctor's decision (5–7 min)

Control station receives 3D model, thermal scan, vitals, and blood results. Doctor communicates via audio and decides next steps.

5

Stabilization and procedures

All actions require doctor confirmation — e.g. glucose for hypoglycemia, soft restraints if aggression returns, robotic shower and warm-air drying for sanitary treatment.

6

Transfer and sterilization

Sealed corridor opens. After patient leaves, both doors lock. Antiseptic spray + high-intensity UV. Ready for next patient in ~15 minutes.

2.3.1 Protocol Delta — aggressive patients

Activated on verbal/physical aggression or psychomotor agitation without response to instructions. Goal: safe, humane preparation for diagnosis.

Stage 1 — Verbal intervention

Doctor via speakers: "Hello. You are in a medical unit. We are here to help you. Please remain calm and lie still." System waits 1–2 minutes.

Stage 2 — Mechanical restraint

Padded cushions rise from bed sides — limit movement without pain or circulation impairment. Purpose: prevent dangerous movements, not inflict pain.

Stage 3 — Inhalation sedation

Controlled sevoflurane or equivalent with elevated oxygen. Patient falls asleep naturally. Continuous SpO2 and breathing monitoring. Doctor can abort anytime.

Stage 4 — Return to standard protocol

Robotic arm performs IV, blood draw, and procedures under full supervision.

2.4 Key components

Robotic manipulator

Flexible, precise, interchangeable tools: gripper, catheter needle, disinfecting swab.

Diagnostic scanner

Thermal imaging and volumetric scanning — fractures, bleeding, abnormalities.

Rapid laboratory

Compact blood analyzer — minutes instead of an hour in a conventional lab.

System "brain"

High-performance computer aggregating all sensor data, presenting clear dashboards, executing doctor commands.

Control station

Doctor works from a secure room: multi-angle video, vitals with auto-highlighted anomalies, voice and touchscreen commands.

Safety infrastructure

LiDAR, HEPA ventilation, UV-C lamps, double-door airlock, floor drainage, remote physician display.

Chapter 3

Implementation in healthcare

3.1 Deployment models

External extension

Prefab unit on foundation next to ER with sealed corridor.

Pros: rapid deployment (weeks), no major reconstruction, cost-effective.

Cons: requires hospital grounds space, may affect appearance.

Built-in integration

Multiple AMMs in new hospital or full ER reconstruction.

Pros: perfect logistics, aesthetic integration.

Cons: high cost, long timeline, new buildings only.

Mobile AMM

Module on truck chassis for disasters, mass casualties, remote areas.

Pros: high flexibility and mobility.

Cons: full autonomy required, higher operating costs.

3.2 Economic and social impact

Direct benefits: lower nosocomial infection treatment costs; reduced workload for nurses, support staff, and security. Indirect benefits: less professional burnout; higher emergency department throughput. Social impact: guaranteed dignified care for people often excluded from the healthcare system.

3.3 New professional roles

  • AMM doctor-operator — emergency or ICU specialist trained on the module interface
  • AMM maintenance engineer — servicing, calibration, repair of modules and sterilization systems

3.4 Ethics and legal aspects

Emergency care without prior consent when life is at immediate risk. Responsibility always with the doctor — the robot is a tool. Reduced direct contact increases chances of rapid, safe, dignified care.

3.5 Limitations, risks, and mitigation

AMM does not replace CT/MRI/ultrasound, final diagnosis, or full-scale resuscitation when standard emergency protocols apply.

Key risks: sensor or manipulator failure; incomplete data without doctor supervision; disinfection cycle failure; patient self-harm or equipment damage; legal/regulatory gaps.

Mitigation: mandatory doctor supervision and full documentation; safe-failure modes (manual override, staff summon, disable hazardous functions); regular maintenance and sterilization verification; comprehensive training and authorization system.

Conclusion

Creating an AMM is a technically achievable medium-term goal. The concept integrates existing robotic and diagnostic technologies into a new admission model — available 24/7, standardizing care and reducing human factors like fear of contaminated patients. Healthcare personnel stay physically separated from aggressive or infectious patients. Engineering, ethical, and legal challenges remain, but AMM represents a realistic path to more humane and effective care for society's most vulnerable.
